- Link: `https://getday60.com/try?utm_source=linkedin&utm_medium=social&utm_campaign=2026-w51-LI-firstline` (last line before the hashtags)

```
The first line of a cold email does most of the work.

Most first lines are about the sender:
"I'm reaching out to express my strong interest in the role."

The reader has seen that line many times this week. Nothing in it is about them.

Try a first line about their team:
"Saw your support team is hiring two leads after the launch."

It shows you read the posting. It names something they care about right now. And it earns the second line, where you add one line of proof that you can help.

Three checks for your first line:
1. Could it only go to this team?
2. Does it name their goal or their problem, not yours?
3. Is it short enough to read on a phone?

(Both lines are illustrative.)

Day60 drafts first lines like this for each of the 4 people closest to the hire, and you rewrite them in your own words.

Today, rewrite the first line of one message you haven't sent yet.

```
In sales development, nobody calls a company.

You call a person. A company can't say yes. A person can.

I founded ClickUp's sales development team, and that's still the idea I'd keep if I could keep only one.

Job seekers get taught the opposite. Apply to the company. Wait for the company. Hope the company writes back.

The company never writes back. A person does, if you give them a reason.

So before your next application, answer two questions:
1. Who would I report to?
2. What problem is that person trying to solve right now?

If you can't answer the second one yet, that's your homework before you write a word.

It's the idea I built Day60 around.

Who do you write to first: the company, or the person?

The five stages of waiting for a reply:

1. Refresh.
2. Refresh again, just in case.
3. Draft a follow-up. Delete it. Too soon.
